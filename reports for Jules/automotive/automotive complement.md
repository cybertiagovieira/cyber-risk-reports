Recurring weaknesses include poor vendor security and legacy OT systems. Automotive plants often lack segmented networks and rely on unpatched systems (WannaCry). Many dealers/dealers’ vendors had weak MFA or delayed patching (FTC Safeguards missed deadlines). Incident response and detection remain weak: breached DMS providers took months to recover. Backup integrity tests and recovery drills are infrequent, leading to high impact when ransomware strikes. Improvement areas include enforcing MFA for all critical systems, automating patch and vulnerability scans, enhancing OT visibility (IEC 62443), bolstering IR planning, and mandating supplier cybersecurity audits (via CSMS/SOMS assessments). Coordination through Auto-ISAC and TISAX shows promise but is uneven across small suppliers.

**Documented cases (disclosed vs estimate):** 

- **Toyota / Kojima (Feb 2022):** Supplier network “system failure” from cyberattack forced Toyota to idle 28 production lines at 14 plants. Toyota did not disclose financials but commentary suggested daily output loss of thousands of vehicles.
- **Honda (June 2020):** Ransomware (Snake) disrupted Honda’s global operations; customer support and financial services were unavailable. In 8-K filings, Honda reported no material revenue loss but noted the “technical difficulties”. Independent analysts estimated ~$50–100M in lost output and recovery costs (models, inc. overtime).
- **Dealership network (Jun 2024):** DMS provider outage caused ~3-week downtime for 15,000 dealers. Industry estimated $1B lost sales/prod. No single company disclosure found; figure from industry analysis.
- **Stellantis (Jan 2025):** Third-party call-center breach leaked 18M customers. Stellantis’ 10-K (2025) notes minor “data exposure” incident; no significant financial impact disclosed. External estimate: potential costs in tens of millions (remediation, fines).
- **Kia (Sept 2025):** Breach at external vendor Malgnsoft leaked 5,000 union-member records. Kia’s disclosures: none reported (vendor notified local agencies); impact limited to employee personal data (encrypted).
- **Kia Motors America (Feb 2021):** Alleged DoppelPaymer demand ~$20M (later denied by Kia). Actual outcome unclear. Undisclosed cost.

Where figures were not published, we rely on reporting or omit them. Many firms only cite *“no material impact”* (e.g. OEMs) – gaps noted.

*Relative Positions:* Automotive is **more exposed** to supply-chain attacks and JIT interruption than most sectors, given global multi-tier supply chains and lean inventories. It is **similarly exposed** to manufacturing (non-auto) in phishing and ransomware risk (both targeted manufacturing). Automotive’s dedicated regulations (R155/R156/GB standards) make it **better governed** in cybersecurity than general manufacturing, but less than finance (which has GLBA/SOX). Automotive has **more OT risk** than finance/retail (like healthcare or utilities) but less than energy networks. In fraud, automotive shows moderate median losses (similar to manufacturing) and is vulnerable to BEC payment fraud via dealers, putting it above average compared to healthcare/energy but below FS (wired fraud). Automotive’s governance complexity (jv’s, captive finance) is higher than in tech or healthcare, implying multifaceted risk management but also diversified risk sharing.

**Confidence:** *Medium*. Data limitations and sector differences (e.g. auto data often folded into “manufacturing”) reduce certainty. The above positions use broad industry indicators; actual automotive-specific studies are scarce, so some inferences are drawn from adjacent manufacturing data. Public-reporting bias (e.g. auto OEMs underreport cyber losses) may understate true risk.

## Subsector Variation and Gaps

- **OEM vs Tier suppliers:** OEMs must meet strict type-approval rules (UN R155/156, NHTSA) and invest heavily in CSMS/OT security. Smaller Tier-n suppliers often lack formal programs (fewer TISAX certifications) and suffer major implications if hit (ex: Toyota 2022). Dealers have limited IT budgets and are often second-line targets (focusing on compliance under FTC Safeguards). Captive finance arms face standard financial regulations (e.g. GLBA, 260-day retention) but also need strong IT controls for loan systems.
- **Geography:** EU OEMs face UN R155/156 and NIS2 mandates; Chinese EV-makers must implement GB 44495/44496 (from Jan 2026). US OEMs follow voluntary NHTSA guidance and must comply with BIS supply-chain rules. Japanese/Korean firms often follow UNECE but lack equivalent national mandates; however, they coordinate via Auto-ISAC Japan. China also enforces data localization, affecting connected services. Brexit/UK: NIS2 still adopting, and UK has no direct vehicle cyber regulation, relying on type-approval mirroring EU.
- **Missing Indicators:** Few public figures exist for “average ransomware downtime in auto sector” or “dealership cyber insurance claims”. Cross-sector fraud data often excludes automotive explicitly. Many automotive firms do not detail cyber losses in SEC filings (aside from “cybersecurity risks” sections), creating evidence gaps.
- **Conflicts/Gaps:** Some media reports (e.g. Kia ransom) are later denied by companies, creating confusion. Different datasets use inconsistent industry codes (auto vs manufacturing vs transport). Many figures are from surveys (IBM, ACFE) with no automotive breakdown. In fraud comparisons, auto often falls under broad “transport” or “wholesale/retail” making direct comparisons imperfect.
