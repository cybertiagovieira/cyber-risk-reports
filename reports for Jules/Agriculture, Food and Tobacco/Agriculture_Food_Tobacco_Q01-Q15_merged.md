## Q01 - Sector Operating Model & Key Dependencies

*Source: File A, Part 1. Conclusion: the sector runs on tightly coupled, just-in-time physical processes controlled by converging IT and OT systems; concentrated processors and distributors, foreign-sourced inputs and legacy OT are the principal single points of failure.*

The Agriculture, Food, and Tobacco (AFT) sector operates on a model of very high efficiency, characterised by tightly integrated supply chains and just-in-time delivery systems that are highly vulnerable to disruption [S-007, S-021]. The modern AFT industry relies heavily on a confluence of IT and OT systems to manage everything from planting and harvesting to processing, distribution, and retail sales [S-002]. This deep technological integration, while driving productivity, has expanded the attack surface and created systemic dependencies that can transmit a cyber incident across numerous organisations and into other key sectors [S-013].

The sector's essential operational processes are fundamentally tied to time-sensitive physical activities. In primary production, this includes planting, irrigation, harvesting, and livestock management, where delays can result in significant crop yield loss or spoiled animal products [S-013]. In food processing and manufacturing, revenue is generated through efficient production runs, packaging, quality control, and cold storage, often under strict food safety regulations [S-016]. Any disruption to these continuous processes can lead to the spoilage of perishable goods, wasted raw materials, and unfulfilled customer orders [S-001, S-042]. For tobacco products, the process involves curing, blending, and manufacturing, which also rely on controlled environments and specialised machinery [S-048]. The entire system is predicated on a complex web of interdependencies. Production depends on energy, water, telecommunications, and agricultural inputs like seeds and fertilisers [S-012, S-020]. Processing facilities depend on logistics companies for just-in-time ingredient delivery and for distributing finished products to retailers [S-007]. Retailers, in turn, depend on sophisticated ordering, payment, and inventory management systems [S-002].

Key IT and OT systems form the nervous system of this operation. In precision agriculture, systems include GPS-guided tractors, drones, and IoT sensors for monitoring soil moisture and crop health, which are vulnerable to spoofing and jamming [S-011, S-021]. In food processing plants, OT systems like Programmable Logic Controllers (PLCs), Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA) platforms, and Human-Machine Interfaces (HMIs) are used to automate production lines and control environmental conditions like temperature and humidity [S-014, S-016]. These are increasingly connected to corporate IT systems like Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) and Warehouse Management Systems (WMS) for scheduling, inventory tracking, and supply chain coordination [S-016]. This convergence is a double-edged sword; it enables real-time visibility and predictive maintenance but also creates a direct pathway for cyber threats to move from less secure IT environments to the more important OT environment [S-014, S-039]. Single points of failure are prevalent. The U.S. meat processing industry is heavily concentrated, with four companies controlling over 80% of beef processing capacity, making the sector highly susceptible to systemic shock if one of these giants is compromised [S-008]. Similarly, large wholesale distributors like United Natural Foods Inc. (UNFI) act as massive logistical hubs, serving tens of thousands of retail locations; an attack on such a distributor can cause widespread shelf shortages and significant financial losses across the retail sector [S-038, S-041].

This dependency matrix reveals a sector built on efficiency at the expense of resilience. The reliance on just-in-time models means there is little buffer stock to absorb disruptions [S-007]. The convergence of IT and OT creates a vast and often poorly understood attack surface, particularly concerning the proliferation of "shadow OT" - networked devices added for efficiency that go unnoticed by traditional IT asset management [S-004]. Furthermore, the sector's heavy reliance on third-party vendors, including Managed Service Providers (MSPs) and software-as-a-service (SaaS) platforms, introduces another layer of risk. Attackers increasingly target these weaker links to gain indirect access to larger, more lucrative targets, a tactic that has proven devastatingly effective against the AFT sector [S-003, S-027]. The operating models, which often involve extensive outsourcing and multi-site operations, can complicate governance and visibility, making it difficult for central leadership to ensure consistent security standards across all parts of the enterprise [S-013]. While complexity alone is not a sign of poor governance, in this context, it amplifies the challenge of securing a distributed and interconnected ecosystem.

## Q02 - Major Cyber Incidents & Case Studies

*Source: File A, Part 2. Conclusion: ransomware dominates; the decision-relevant events are JBS (2021), UNFI (2025), Asahi (2025), M&S (2025) and the October 2026 European refrigeration attack, which together show concentration, distributor chokepoints, prolonged recovery, earnings impact and physical destruction of stock.*

The Agriculture, Food, and Tobacco (AFT) sector has experienced a dramatic escalation in the frequency and severity of cyberattacks over the past several years, with ransomware emerging as the dominant threat vector [S-004, S-005]. Between 2024 and July 2026, ransomware attacks targeting the sector increased by 62%, and the total number of such incidents tracked by the Food and Ag-ISAC surged 82% in 2025 alone [S-004, S-007]. This trend reflects a deliberate shift by attackers who recognise the sector's structural vulnerabilities: its time-sensitive, just-in-time operational model creates immense pressure to restore services quickly, making ransom payments more likely [S-005, S-039]. The FBI has specifically warned that agricultural cooperatives are lucrative targets due to their vital role in the supply chain and their willingness to pay ransoms to avoid disruption during key planting and harvest seasons [S-006, S-039].

Based on financial materiality and operational disruption severity, the top five most decision-relevant incidents are:

1.  **JBS Foods (2021)**: This attack serves as the quintessential example of systemic risk. It demonstrated that a single ransomware attack on the world's largest meat processor could halt nearly one-fifth of U.S. beef processing capacity, causing market-wide price volatility and forcing a multi-million dollar ransom payment [S-008, S-009]. Its selection rationale is its clear demonstration of maximum possible impact on a concentrated industry.
2.  **United Natural Foods (UNFI) (2025)**: This incident highlights the high fragility of the modern food distribution network. An attack on a single distributor caused an estimated $350-$400 million in lost sales and disrupted operations for over 30,000 retailers, illustrating how a chokepoint in the supply chain can amplify a localised attack into a broad market shock [S-025, S-038].
3.  **Asahi Group Holdings (2025)**: This case is notable for the long-term operational disruption and refusal to pay the ransom. The attack crippled production across 30 factories for months, leading to significant revenue and profit losses, and resulted in the potential exposure of 1.9 million customer records, showcasing both the physical and reputational damage [S-025, S-045].
4.  **Marks and Spencer (M&S) (2025)**: This attack demonstrates the direct impact of a cyber incident on a major retailer's bottom line. The disruption to online and in-store operations led to a staggering £324 million in lost sales and a 55.4% drop in pre-tax profits, providing a clear, quantified example of the financial transmission from cyber incident to credit risk [S-045].
5.  **European Supermarket Refrigeration Hack (2026)**: This incident is a stark, undeniable proof-of-concept of a cyberattack causing direct physical destruction of assets. By manipulating firmware in internet-exposed refrigerators, attackers destroyed millions of euros' worth of perishable inventory, moving the threat from data encryption to tangible product loss [S-043].

### Detailed Case Study: JBS Foods (2021)

*   **Why Selected:** To illustrate the systemic risk posed by the concentration of the U.S. meat processing industry and the effectiveness of ransomware as a tool for causing physical-world disruption.
*   **Timeline:**
    *   **May 29-30, 2021:** The Russian-linked REvil ransomware group attacks JBS's IT systems [S-009].
    *   **May 30, 2021:** JBS suspends computer systems at its North American and Australian facilities, forcing the closure of all U.S. meat plants [S-008].
    *   **June 2021:** JBS confirms it paid an $11 million ransom to the attackers to expedite recovery and mitigate further issues [S-008, S-009].
    *   **July 13, 2021:** Following a call between the U.S. and Russian presidents, the REvil group's infrastructure went offline [S-009].
*   **Initial Access & Mechanism:** The initial access vector was not publicly specified by JBS, though it is suspected to be via compromised credentials or a phishing campaign common in ransomware attacks. The mechanism was a sophisticated ransomware deployment that encrypted files and disabled business-essential systems, forcing a shutdown of physical processing plants [S-008, S-010].
*   **Exploited Weaknesses:** The attack exploited the fundamental business reality that production cannot run without functional IT systems for scheduling, weighing, and quality control. The company's own statement noted that the attack was so sophisticated that it necessitated paying the ransom to prevent unforeseen issues and data leaks [S-008].
*   **Affected Services:** Corporate IT systems, business management software, and operational support systems were crippled. This indirectly halted all cattle and pig slaughtering operations at its U.S. facilities [S-008].
*   **Operational & Financial Effects:** U.S. cattle slaughter fell 22% in the week of the attack, and pork slaughter was down 20% [S-008]. The disruption caused Chicago cattle futures to plummet and prompted the USDA to encourage other companies to increase production to fill the gap [S-008, S-009]. Financially, the company paid an $11 million ransom and faced significant market uncertainty [S-009].
*   **Recovery & Lessons:** JBS credited its robust cybersecurity protocols, including redundant systems and encrypted backup servers, for allowing a quick resolution [S-008]. The key lesson is that even well-resourced firms are vulnerable, and the concentration of essential infrastructure in a few hands creates a systemic vulnerability that can have macroeconomic consequences.

### Detailed Case Study: United Natural Foods (UNFI) (2025)

*   **Why Selected:** To demonstrate the domino effect of a cyberattack originating in a midstream distributor and impacting thousands of downstream retailers and consumers.
*   **Timeline:**
    *   **June 5, 2025:** UNFI detects a cybersecurity incident and takes affected IT systems offline [S-038].
    *   **Mid-June 2025:** Manual workarounds are implemented, but order fulfilment is severely hampered, leading to empty shelves at retail stores [S-040].
    *   **Q4 2025:** UNFI discloses in an SEC filing that the attack will have a material financial impact and expects to lose sales [S-038].
*   **Initial Access & Mechanism:** The attack began with social engineering followed by a ransomware deployment. The exact initial access method remains undisclosed, but it allowed attackers to gain deep access into UNFI's core IT systems [S-025, S-038].
*   **Exploited Weaknesses:** The attack exploited the inherent vulnerability of a single point of failure in a key logistical hub. With 53 distribution centres serving approximately 30,000 customers, the compromise of the central IT and WMS platforms brought the entire operation to a standstill [S-025, S-038].
*   **Affected Services:** Electronic ordering and invoicing systems, warehouse management, and distribution logistics were all taken offline [S-045].
*   **Operational & Financial Effects:** The disruption caused delayed deliveries, product spoilage, and empty shelves at major retailers like Whole Foods [S-038, S-040]. The financial impact was severe, with estimates of lost sales reaching up to $400 million and a reduction in quarterly earnings [S-001, S-041].
*   **Recovery & Lessons:** UNFI relied on manual processes for several weeks before fully restoring its systems [S-038]. The incident underscores the high fragility of just-in-time supply chains and the outsized risk posed by large, centralised distributors. For lenders, it highlights the importance of assessing a borrower's position within its supply chain and the resilience of its key logistical partners.

### Detailed Case Study: European Supermarket Refrigeration Hack (2026)

*   **Why Selected:** To provide a clear example of a cyberattack with direct, measurable physical consequences, moving beyond data encryption to the destruction of physical assets.
*   **Timeline:**
    *   **October 2026:** A coordinated cyberattack occurs overnight across multiple European countries.
    *   **October 2026:** Victims report that refrigeration systems in over 700 stores have failed, destroying perishable goods [S-043].
*   **Initial Access & Mechanism:** Attackers gained remote access to commercial refrigeration controllers by exploiting unpatched firmware vulnerabilities (CVE-2025-41450, CVE-2025-41452 for Danfoss; 23 flaws for Dixell/Copeland) in devices managed by third-party service providers. They manipulated defrost cycles to hide their activity before raising temperatures to destructive levels [S-043].
*   **Exploited Weaknesses:** The primary weakness was the use of end-of-life firmware on internet-exposed OT devices. The rapid weaponisation of public exploit details (a dangerously short window) highlights the difficulty of patching legacy industrial equipment [S-043]. Poor device hygiene and a lack of visibility into third-party managed OT assets were also contributing factors.
*   **Affected Services:** The physical integrity of the cold chain was compromised in over 700 grocery stores. Environmental monitoring systems and alarms were disabled or manipulated [S-043].
*   **Operational & Financial Effects:** Perishable inventory, including food items, was destroyed. Temperatures were forced above 50°C, causing fires in some cases. The geographical spread across France, Spain, Poland, Germany, and Belgium indicates a mass compromise of a widely used piece of hardware [S-043]. Reported losses were in the tens of millions of euros [S-043].
*   **Recovery & Lessons:** The incident demonstrates that OT systems are not immune to cyber threats and that a digital attack can have immediate and costly physical consequences. It serves as a powerful reminder that for any organisation relying on cold chain logistics, the security of embedded controllers and firmware is as important as protecting corporate IT systems. The lesson for credit officers is that physical asset destruction resulting from a cyberattack can lead to direct, uninsured losses far exceeding typical ransom demands.

## Q03 - Cyber Threat Landscape & Attack Scenarios

*Source: File A, Part 3. Conclusion: three scenarios dominate - third-party supply-chain compromise (confidence High), targeted OT disruption (Medium to High) and geopolitically motivated espionage and sabotage (High for espionage; increasing for sabotage).*

The cyber threat landscape facing the Agriculture, Food, and Tobacco (AFT) sector is dominated by financially motivated cybercriminals, primarily ransomware groups, but is increasingly complicated by state-sponsored actors pursuing strategic objectives. Analysis of documented incidents and threat intelligence reveals three prioritised threat scenarios that pose the greatest risk to the sector's operational and financial stability. These scenarios are not mutually exclusive and often overlap, with threat actors frequently combining techniques from multiple vectors.

**Scenario 1: Third-Party Supply-Chain Compromise**

*   **Mechanism:** This scenario involves attackers bypassing a large, well-defended AFT organisation by instead targeting a less-secure third-party vendor, Managed Service Provider (MSP), or software platform that has legitimate access to the target's systems. This approach exploits the principle of least privilege at a supply chain level, turning trusted relationships into attack vectors.
*   **Threat Actors & Motivation:** Primarily ransomware-as-a-service (RaaS) groups like Clop, Qilin, and Akira, who are financially motivated and seek to maximise their impact and extortion leverage by attacking entire ecosystems rather than isolated targets [S-034, S-058]. Nation-state actors may also use this method as a proxy to achieve plausible deniability [S-040].
*   **Attack Pathway:**
    1.  **Initial Access:** The attacker identifies a vulnerable third party (e.g., a small logistics firm, a software vendor with weak security) and gains access via phishing, exploiting a vulnerability in their software, or using stolen credentials [S-003, S-027].
    2.  **Lateral Movement/Persistence:** Once inside the third-party network, the attacker establishes persistence and maps the environment to find its connections to higher-value AFT clients.
    3.  **Compromise of Target:** Using the legitimate access credentials or access mechanisms provided by the compromised vendor, the attacker pivots to the AFT organisation's network.
    4.  **Execution & Impact:** The attacker deploys ransomware or exfiltrates data, causing disruption to the AFT client's operations.
*   **Exploited Weaknesses:**
    *   **Inadequate Third-Party Risk Management (TPRM):** A pervasive weakness is the failure of AFT organisations to rigorously vet the security posture of their suppliers. One survey found 54% of manufacturers do not vet third-party security before granting access [S-003]. Over 42% of manufacturing breaches occur via a third party [S-003].
    *   **Shared Technology Platforms:** Compromise of a shared SaaS platform or file transfer service can have cascading effects. The exploitation of Cleo's managed file transfer software by the Clop group in 2024-2025 affected hundreds of victims, including Hertz and WK Kellogg Co. [S-034, S-058, S-059].
    *   **Permanent Remote Access Paths:** Many vendors maintain permanent remote access to client networks for support, creating a persistent backdoor if the vendor's security is breached [S-003].
*   **Evidence & Confidence:** This scenario is **confirmed** and is a leading attack pattern. The UNFI attack originated from a business management software company [S-006]; the Clop-led attacks on Cleo affected numerous manufacturing and wholesale clients [S-034]; and the attack on M&S involved a third-party provider being tricked into resetting a password [S-045]. Confidence in this threat is **High**.

**Scenario 2: Targeted Operational Technology (OT) Disruption**

*   **Mechanism:** This scenario involves attackers specifically targeting and compromising OT/ICS systems to cause physical disruption to production, processing, or storage. The goal is not necessarily data theft or financial extortion through encryption, but rather to halt operations, destroy physical assets, or manipulate products.
*   **Threat Actors & Motivation:** This is driven by a mix of financially motivated ransomware groups seeking to maximise downtime leverage and nation-state actors with disruptive or destructive intentions (wiper malware). Groups like Dark Angels have demonstrated capability in this area [S-004, S-014]. Pro-Russia hacktivists have also shown intent to target HMIs to halt production lines [S-004].
*   **Attack Pathway:**
    1.  **Initial Access (via IT):** Access is typically gained through the corporate IT network via phishing, exploiting a vulnerability, or using stolen credentials. OT networks are rarely internet-facing [S-039].
    2.  **Network Reconnaissance & Mapping:** The attacker moves laterally from the IT network into the OT network, mapping the Purdue Architecture levels to locate key PLCs, HMIs, and SCADA systems [S-014, S-056].
    3.  **Exploitation of OT Vulnerabilities:** Attackers exploit weaknesses in OT protocols (e.g., Modbus, DNP3) or use malware designed to interact with ICS components (e.g., EKANS/Snake) to send malicious commands [S-014].
    4.  **Execution of Physical Impact:** The attacker manipulates the physical process - for example, by changing setpoints on a PLC to alter chemical mixing ratios, forcing motors to run at unsafe speeds, or manipulating a refrigeration controller to raise temperatures [S-011, S-043].
*   **Exploited Weaknesses:**
    *   **Poor IT/OT Segmentation:** The most important weakness is the lack of proper network segmentation. Flat networks allow threats to move freely from the corporate IT environment to the production OT floor [S-003, S-039].
    *   **Legacy and Unpatched OT Systems:** Many OT devices run on old, unsupported operating systems and use proprietary protocols that lack basic security features like authentication or encryption. Patching is very challenging due to operational risk [S-014, S-039].
    *   **Lack of OT Visibility:** Organisations often have poor visibility into their own OT assets ("shadow OT"), meaning they don't know what devices are on their network, what software they run, or what ports are open, making detection nearly impossible [S-004, S-056].
    *   **Default Credentials & Unsafe Practices:** IoT sensors and other OT devices often ship with default passwords that are never changed, providing an easy entry point for attackers [S-016].
*   **Evidence & Confidence:** This scenario is **confirmed**, though less common than supply-chain attacks. The 2026 European supermarket refrigeration hack is a prime example of physical destruction via OT manipulation [S-043]. The FBI has warned of attacks on grain cooperatives timed for harvest season [S-006], and researchers have demonstrated remote tractor hijacking [S-021]. Confidence in this threat is **Medium to High**, with the likelihood increasing as attackers become more sophisticated.

**Scenario 3: Geopolitically Motivated Espionage and Sabotage**

*   **Mechanism:** This scenario involves state-sponsored or state-aligned actors conducting cyber operations against the AFT sector for strategic purposes, such as stealing intellectual property (IP), gathering intelligence on supply chain vulnerabilities, or preparing for future disruptive attacks.
*   **Threat Actors & Motivation:** Nation-states including China, Russia, Iran, and North Korea. Their motivations are strategic and long-term: China seeks IP for food security; Russia and Iran engage in reconnaissance of essential infrastructure; North Korea uses cyber operations to fund its regime [S-004, S-013, S-036].
*   **Attack Pathway:**
    1.  **Pre-positioning & Reconnaissance:** Long-term, patient campaigns involving spear-phishing emails and the exploitation of zero-day vulnerabilities to gain a foothold in target networks without triggering alarms [S-013, S-057].
    2.  **Data Exfiltration:** Steady, low-and-slow exfiltration of sensitive data, such as proprietary seed genetics, biotechnology research, or lists of key supply chain partners [S-021, S-058].
    3.  **Infrastructure Mapping:** Systematic mapping of the target's IT and OT architecture to understand dependencies and identify potential chokepoints for future disruption [S-013].
*   **Exploited Weaknesses:**
    *   **Sophisticated Spear-Phishing:** State actors use highly targeted and convincing phishing campaigns, often impersonating trusted entities or leveraging insider knowledge [S-033].
    *   **Zero-Day Vulnerabilities:** These actors have the resources to discover and weaponise previously unknown software vulnerabilities, giving them a significant advantage over defenders [S-027].
    *   **Insufficient Advanced Threat Detection:** Defenders may lack the tools and expertise to detect the subtle, long-term presence of advanced persistent threat (APT) actors within their networks [S-013].
*   **Evidence & Confidence:** This scenario is **confirmed**. Chinese APT groups have been attributed with stealing data from ag-tech firms like Trimble Inc. and biosecurity platforms like USAHerds [S-021, S-058]. Russian agents have mapped U.S. food supply-chain dependencies, and Iranian operators have targeted irrigation systems [S-013]. Confidence in this threat is **High**, particularly for espionage, and **Increasing** for sabotage, as the lines between state actors and criminal groups blur [S-037].

## Q04 - Control Weaknesses, Resilience Capabilities & Benchmarks

*Source: File A, Part 4. Conclusion: IAM, IT/OT segmentation, asset visibility, tested offline backups and OT threat detection are the highest-priority capabilities; the documented gap to NIST CSF and ISA/IEC 62443 benchmarks is wide, especially for smaller producers and legacy OT.*

While large multinational corporations in the Agriculture, Food, and Tobacco (AFT) sector invest significantly in cybersecurity - JBS, for instance, spends over $200 million annually - the sector as a whole exhibits a fragmented and often inadequate cybersecurity posture [S-008]. This is largely due to the prevalence of smaller producers and processors operating on thin margins, coupled with the inherent challenges of securing legacy Operational Technology (OT) environments [S-013, S-039]. A comparative analysis of best practices, such as the NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) and the ISA/IEC 62443 standard for OT, against documented industry weaknesses reveals significant gaps that expose borrowers to heightened credit risk [S-031, S-032]. The highest-priority resilience capabilities are those that address the most common attack pathways identified in Part 3, namely supply-chain compromise and OT infiltration.

First and foremost is **Identity and Access Management (IAM)**. Enforcing strong controls around who can access what systems is a foundational defence. This includes implementing multi-factor authentication (MFA) universally across all platforms, from email and financial systems to cloud applications and logistics portals [S-050]. Privileged access must be strictly controlled and monitored, as compromised admin credentials are a common initial access vector for ransomware gangs [S-026]. The absence of these controls was a contributing factor in numerous incidents, including the attack on Marks & Spencer, where attackers impersonated an employee to convince a third-party provider to reset a password [S-045]. IAM is considered recommended good practice for all organisations, but its importance is elevated for AFT companies due to their extensive third-party interactions.

Second, **Network Segmentation** is arguably the most important technical control for protecting OT environments. Given that many attacks originate in the corporate IT network and pivot to OT, a properly segmented architecture acts as a firewall [S-014, S-039]. Based on the Purdue Enterprise Reference Architecture (PERA), traffic should only be allowed to flow between adjacent network levels, with a hardened Industrial DMZ (iDMZ) serving as the sole conduit between the general IT network (Levels 4-5) and the OT environment (Levels 0-3) [S-014]. This prevents ransomware, which typically propagates through IT protocols like SMB, from reaching and encrypting PLCs and HMIs. Despite its importance, many food processing sites operate with flat, unsegmented networks, creating a direct path for threats to move from a compromised Windows PC running a SCADA interface to the underlying physical control systems [S-003, S-015].

Third, achieving comprehensive **Asset Visibility and Inventory** is a prerequisite for any effective security programme. Many organisations in the sector suffer from a "shadow OT" problem, where networked devices like IoT sensors, automated feeders, or cold chain thermometers are added to improve efficiency but are never inventoried or secured by the IT department [S-004]. Without knowing what devices are on the network, it is impossible to patch them, monitor their traffic, or defend them against attack. This lack of visibility was a key challenge highlighted in CISA's research and was a contributing factor in the European refrigeration hack, where attackers exploited internet-exposed controllers that were likely unknown to the retailers' security teams [S-043, S-051].

Fourth, **Backup Integrity and Recovery Testing** are essential for ensuring business continuity. A robust backup strategy involves regular, automated backups of key data and systems, stored in a secure, offline, or air-gapped location to protect them from ransomware encryption. However, simply having backups is not enough; they must be tested regularly to ensure they can be restored successfully under realistic conditions. The failure to test backups was a likely reason for the extended downtime in the Asahi Group Holdings attack, which lasted for months [S-045]. Conversely, JBS's ability to recover relatively quickly was attributed to its redundant systems and encrypted backup servers that were not affected by the attack [S-008]. This capability is a mandatory requirement under frameworks like NIS2 and a key expectation for insurers.

Finally, **Threat Detection and Response** capabilities, particularly within OT environments, are severely lacking. According to a Dragos report, 88% of OT networks struggle to effectively detect and respond to threats [S-003]. The average dwell time (time from compromise to detection) for ransomware in the Food & Beverage sector is 42 days, compared to just five days for organisations with comprehensive OT visibility [S-045]. This lag allows attackers to move laterally, escalate privileges, and cause maximum damage before being discovered. Implementing specialised OT security monitoring tools that can understand industrial protocols is a key improvement area.

## Q05 - Financial Impact, Disruption & Credit Transmission

*Source: File A, Part 5. Conclusion: cyber losses transmit through lost sales and spoiled perishable stock into EBITDA, working capital and covenants; AFT-specific aggregate statistics are scarce, so manufacturing proxies and company disclosures carry the quantitative case.*

Cyber incidents in the Agriculture, Food, and Tobacco (AFT) sector have demonstrably severe financial consequences, extending far beyond the initial cost of the attack to impact nearly every facet of a company's financial health and creditworthiness. The unique characteristic of the sector, where operational downtime directly correlates with the loss of physical, perishable inventory, makes the financial transmission of a cyber incident particularly acute and damaging. While reliable, aggregated statistics specific to the AFT sector are scarce due to inconsistent reporting, a synthesis of disclosed case studies and broader manufacturing data reveals a clear pattern of significant financial harm.

Quantitative metrics for the AFT sector are limited, but available data provides a glimpse into the scale of the threat. In 2025, the Food and Ag-ISAC recorded 265 ransomware attacks against the sector, representing 4.2% of the total ransomware volume across all key sectors tracked globally [S-007]. The median cost of a manufacturing ransomware attack responded to by Arctic Wolf was $500,000 USD [S-001]. Average ransom demands in the European industrial sector reached $1.16 million in Q3 2025, more than double the previous year [S-026]. Ransomware attacks on the global manufacturing sector surged by 56% in 2025, accounting for roughly half of all documented global attacks that year [S-026]. While these figures are from the broader manufacturing sector, they are highly representative of the AFT subsector's risk profile. More telling are the documented impacts from specific incidents. Clorox, a major consumer goods manufacturer, saw its sales decline by 20% due to a breach, costing the company an estimated $356 million USD [S-001]. Mondelez International's 2017 NotPetya attack, which impacted its global production, resulted in a total financial impact of over $100 million, though its insurer denied the claim under a 'war exclusion' clause [S-010]. MolsonCoors reported attack-related costs of at least $2 million from a brief production halt [S-010].

The financial transmission from a cyber incident to a borrower's credit profile follows a clear causal chain. The initial operational disruption - such as a halt in production lines or the disabling of a distribution centre - leads directly to lost sales and lower revenue [S-001, S-038]. This directly impacts EBITDA and profitability margins. For example, the JBS attack, while resolved quickly, caused a temporary spike in wholesale meat prices and significant market volatility, demonstrating the immediate impact on commodity markets [S-008, S-009]. A more prolonged disruption, like that suffered by Asahi Group Holdings, which refused to pay a ransom, led to months of system downtime and an estimated $31 million in lost revenue and $12.6 million in lost profit [S-045]. Marks and Spencer's attack had an even more dramatic effect, cutting its pre-tax profits by 55.4% to £184.1 million and resulting in £324 million in lost sales [S-045].

Beyond direct revenue loss, the incident triggers a cascade of other financial consequences. Remediation and recovery efforts, including hiring forensic experts, rebuilding systems, and enhancing security, represent a significant capital expenditure and drain on working capital [S-001, S-038]. The need to spend an additional $25 million to secure its systems post-breach was a direct cost for Clorox [S-001]. Regulatory investigations and potential fines add to the burden; while none were reported in the analysed cases, the upcoming requirements of NIS2 and SEC rules will likely change this dynamic [S-024, S-052]. Reputational damage can lead to a loss of customer trust, making it harder to retain existing contracts and acquire new ones, which impacts future borrowing needs and covenant headroom [S-015]. Insurance coverage is often insufficient, as policies may exclude certain types of attacks (like those deemed acts of war) or fail to cover consequential business interruption losses [S-010]. The UNFI attack, for instance, was expected to have a material financial impact, but the full extent remained under evaluation, highlighting the difficulty in quantifying all associated costs [S-038].

These scenarios are not forecasts but are designed to highlight the key assumptions and credit channels that would need to be assessed for a specific borrower.

## Q06 - Regulation & Resilience Expectations

*Source: File A, Part 6. Conclusion: NIS2 (in force) and the SEC disclosure rules (effective 2023) apply now; the UK CS&R Bill is a proposal; together they add reporting deadlines, supply-chain duties and penalty exposure. File B adds food-fraud, failure-to-prevent-fraud and financial-sector comparator frameworks.*

The regulatory landscape for cybersecurity is undergoing a significant and rapid evolution, transitioning from a framework of voluntary guidelines to a regime of legally mandated requirements with severe financial penalties. For the Agriculture, Food, and Tobacco (AFT) sector, this shift is particularly impactful as it is increasingly being recognised as an essential infrastructure component. Jurisdictions across the globe, notably the European Union, the United Kingdom, and the United States, are implementing stringent new rules that will fundamentally alter how AFT companies manage cyber risk, conduct business, and report incidents. These regulations directly influence a borrower's credit risk profile by introducing new compliance costs, potential liabilities, and explicit expectations for operational resilience.

In the **European Union**, the cornerstone of the new regulatory regime is the **NIS2 Directive (Directive (EU) 2022/2555)**. Effective since October 2024, NIS2 significantly broadens the scope of its predecessor and explicitly includes the food sector under the category of "Important Entities" [S-024, S-028]. This designation applies to entities involved in the manufacture, production, and distribution of food. The directive imposes a comprehensive set of obligations, including conducting regular risk assessments that specifically account for threats like physical attacks on food infrastructure, implementing robust minimum security measures, and establishing effective incident detection, analysis, and response capabilities [S-023, S-024]. Perhaps most importantly, NIS2 mandates that Important Entities report any "significant incident" to their national competent authority within 24 hours of having become aware of it, with a full report due within 72 hours [S-029]. Furthermore, they must notify affected customers "without undue delay" [S-018]. Non-compliance can result in substantial fines of up to €10 million or 2% of the undertaking's total worldwide annual turnover from the preceding financial year [S-024]. Complementing NIS2 is the **Cyber Resilience Act (CRA)**, which will apply to all products with digital elements placed on the EU market, including IoT devices used in agriculture and food processing [S-024]. The CRA mandates "secure-by-design" principles, requiring manufacturers to ensure their products are resilient to cyber threats throughout their lifecycle, with ongoing updates and strong cryptographic protections [S-024]. Together, NIS2 and the CRA create a multi-layered regulatory framework that places a heavy burden on AFT companies to demonstrate technical and organisational resilience.

The **United Kingdom** is also strengthening its cybersecurity laws with the introduction of the **Cyber Security and Resilience Bill**. Expected to receive Royal Assent in 2026, this bill replaces the older NIS Regulations 2018 and introduces several key changes that will impact the AFT sector [S-018, S-019]. Crucially, the proposed legislation explicitly includes the "food" sector as an essential service within the "data infrastructure" sub-sector [S-018]. A significant expansion of scope targets "Relevant Managed Service Providers" (RMSPs) - providers who offer ongoing IT system management services to other organisations. These MSPs will be directly regulated, making them responsible for the security of the systems they manage, thereby addressing a major attack vector [S-017, S-018]. The bill also introduces a new designated supplier regime (official term contains a restricted word), allowing regulators to mandate stronger security controls for suppliers whose failure could cause significant disruption [S-018]. The incident notification requirement mirrors the EU's, mandating an initial notification within 24 hours and a full report within 72 hours [S-019]. Penalties for non-compliance are tiered and can be severe, reaching the greater of £10 million or 2% of worldwide annual turnover for lower-tier failures, and the greater of £17 million or 4% for higher-tier failures like security breaches or notification failures [S-018]. These provisions signal a move towards holding the entire supply chain ecosystem accountable for cyber resilience.

In the **United States**, the primary recent development has been the implementation of new **SEC Cybersecurity Disclosure Rules**. Adopted in July 2023, these rules require all SEC registrants, including publicly traded AFT companies, to disclose material cybersecurity incidents on Form 8-K [S-053, S-054]. The disclosure must be made within four business days of determining that the incident is "material" [S-052]. The definition of materiality is investor-focused, considering whether the disclosure would be important to a reasonable investor, and includes qualitative factors like reputational harm and the potential for litigation, not just quantitative financial loss [S-055]. For annual reports on Form 10-K, companies must now provide detailed disclosures about their cybersecurity risk management strategy, governance, and incident response processes under Item 106 [S-053]. These rules, which became effective for most registrants in late 2023, significantly increase transparency and force companies to formally assess and report on their cyber risk. While not a direct operational resilience mandate like NIS2, the SEC rules create a powerful incentive for boards and management to give cybersecurity priority, as failure to do so can lead to shareholder lawsuits and regulatory action from the SEC itself. For lenders, these disclosures provide valuable, albeit sometimes delayed, insight into a borrower's actual risk events and governance maturity.

## Q07 - Geopolitical Cyber Threats & Systemic Exposure

*Source: File A, Part 7. Conclusion: China (espionage), Russia and Iran (reconnaissance and sabotage) and North Korea (revenue-driven ransomware) target the sector; propagation runs through foreign-sourced inputs (rare earths, potash) and logistics chokepoints.*

The cyber threat environment for the Agriculture, Food, and Tobacco (AFT) sector is no longer solely populated by financially motivated cybercriminals. It is increasingly influenced by the strategic interests of nation-states, which view the sector's complex global supply chains as both a source of valuable intelligence and a potential instrument of coercion. Geopolitical tensions are amplifying cyber risks, introducing a layer of strategic uncertainty that can lead to systemic disruptions independent of ransom demands. State-sponsored actors from Russia, China, Iran, and North Korea are actively probing, compromising, and in some cases, sabotaging systems related to food production and distribution [S-004, S-013].

The motivations behind these activities are varied and strategic. **China** is primarily focused on **cyber-enabled espionage** to steal intellectual property (IP) related to agricultural biotechnology, seed genetics, and advanced farming chemicals [S-004, S-021]. By acquiring this IP, Beijing aims to bolster its own domestic food security and agricultural competitiveness. Evidence of this activity includes the compromise of Trimble Inc., a GPS-focused ag-tech firm, by the APT3 group in 2016, and the breach of USAHerds' biosecurity platform by the China-linked group APT41 in 2022 [S-021]. The acquisition of Syngenta by China's ChemChina in 2017 further solidified China's control over vast swaths of global seed genetics and crop protection technology, raising concerns about access to proprietary agronomic data [S-021].

**Russia** and **Iran** appear to be more focused on **reconnaissance and sabotage**. Russian agents have been observed mapping U.S. agricultural and food supply-chain dependencies to identify potential chokepoints that could be exploited during a crisis [S-013]. There is also evidence of Russian military intelligence units targeting grain producer networks with file-encrypting malware, suggesting a readiness to disrupt food supplies as part of broader conflict strategies [S-044]. Iranian cyber actors have specifically targeted programmable logic controllers (PLCs) and industrial control systems supporting water, irrigation, and agricultural environments, indicating an interest in gaining the capability to disrupt physical processes [S-013, S-036]. These activities are not merely preparatory; they can have immediate, destabilising effects. For example, a cyberattack on an Israeli irrigation system damaged controllers and left regions dysfunctional, demonstrating the potential for direct physical impact [S-044].

**North Korea** represents a different kind of threat, characterised by the fusion of state-sponsored hacking with criminal enterprise. North Korean state-sponsored groups have been observed deploying ransomware payloads, such as those from the Qilin group, blurring the lines between espionage, extortion, and outright sabotage [S-037]. Their primary motivation is to generate illicit revenue for the regime, often through fraudulent remote worker schemes or by extorting payments from victims [S-036, S-037]. This hybrid approach makes attribution and response more complex, as it is difficult to distinguish a purely criminal attack from one with strategic intent.

The propagation of these geopolitical threats occurs through the very same dependencies that make the AFT sector vulnerable to criminal actors. The global nature of the food supply chain means that a successful attack on a key input or logistical node can have cascading effects worldwide. The U.S. food system is highly dependent on foreign-sourced inputs, including rare earth minerals from China (for electronics) and potash fertilisers from Russia and Belarus [S-021]. China's control over nearly 90% of the world's rare earth refining and its imposition of fertiliser export bans have already demonstrated the power to cause global price shocks and disrupt farming [S-020, S-021]. A cyberattack on a key piece of infrastructure, such as the Strait of Hormuz, which handles a third of global fertiliser trade, could trigger a structurally more severe food crisis than previous shocks, with fertiliser prices surging and planting decisions being altered globally [S-022].

### Detailed Mapping Entries

**PROV-001: Third-Party Risk Management (TPRM)**

*   **Potential Borrower Weakness:** An adverse answer would indicate that the borrower's TPRM programme is immature or non-existent. This could manifest as a lack of standardised questionnaires for vendors, infrequent security audits, or a failure to enforce contractual security requirements.
*   **Related Threats/Incidents:** This directly enables the "Third-Party Supply-Chain Compromise" scenario. The attack on United Natural Foods (UNFI), which originated from a compromised business management software company, is a canonical example of what happens when TPRM fails [S-006, S-038]. The attack on M&S, where a third-party provider was tricked into resetting a password, further illustrates this vulnerability [S-045].
*   **Dependencies & Regulations:** The borrower's business model likely relies heavily on a network of distributors, logistics providers, and MSPs. Under the EU's NIS2 directive, which covers the food sector, a failure in the supply chain is a direct violation of the minimum security measures that must be implemented [S-023, S-024].
*   **Plausible Consequences:** Operationally, this could lead to a cascading failure where a breach at a partner causes the borrower's systems to go down. Financially, this results in lost sales, penalties for unfulfilled contracts, and reputational damage. From a credit perspective, this exposes the borrower to systemic risk beyond its own control.
*   **Non-Technical Follow-Up Questions:**
    1.  "Can you describe your process for evaluating the cybersecurity risk of a new, key logistics partner before you begin doing business with them?"
    2.  "How do you ensure that your Managed Service Provider has adequate security controls, especially given that they have administrative access to your systems?"
    3.  "Have you ever refused to do business with a vendor because they were unable or unwilling to meet your security requirements?"
*   **Indicators for Specialist Review:** The borrower states that it relies solely on the certifications of its vendors without performing its own checks, or admits that it has no formal process for reviewing the security of its MSPs.

**PROV-002: IT/OT Network Segmentation**

*   **Potential Borrower Weakness:** An adverse answer would suggest that the borrower's IT and OT networks are not properly isolated. This could be evidenced by a lack of firewalls between network zones or the use of common protocols that allow unrestricted communication.
*   **Related Threats/Incidents:** This is the primary enabler of the "Targeted OT Disruption" scenario. The 2026 attack on European supermarkets, where hackers manipulated refrigeration controllers, would have been much harder if the HVAC systems had been on a segregated OT network [S-043]. The Colonial Pipeline attack, while in a different sector, is a classic example of what happens when a VPN (IT perimeter) provides unfettered access to the core operational network [S-014].
*   **Dependencies & Regulations:** The borrower's modern operations almost certainly involve the convergence of IT systems (like ERP) with OT systems (like PLCs and SCADA) [S-016]. While not a direct regulation, failing to segment these networks goes against the consensus best practice embodied in frameworks like NIST SP 800-82 and IEC 62443, and would be viewed negatively by auditors and insurers.
*   **Plausible Consequences:** Operationally, this leaves production lines, cold storage, and other key physical processes directly exposed to threats originating in the corporate IT network. A ransomware infection on a manager's laptop could theoretically spread to the PLC controlling a production line. This could lead to catastrophic downtime and physical asset damage.
*   **Non-Technical Follow-Up Questions:**
    1.  "Can you walk me through the network architecture between your main office computers and the systems that control your production floor?"
    2.  "How do you prevent a cyber incident on your email server or finance system from being able to impact the machines that run your manufacturing equipment?"
    3.  "What is your process for approving any new connection between your IT network and your OT network?"
*   **Indicators for Specialist Review:** The borrower describes its networks as "flat" or admits that vendors have direct access from the internet to production systems. They mention that testing network changes is too risky for production.

**PROV-003: Asset Visibility**

*   **Potential Borrower Weakness:** An adverse answer would mean the borrower does not know the full extent of its connected devices. This could be because they manually track only "approved" assets and are unaware of "shadow IT/OT" added by individual departments.
*   **Related Threats/Incidents:** This weakness is foundational to all scenarios, as attackers always prefer to target unknown, unmonitored assets. The European refrigeration hack succeeded in part because the attackers knew of a vulnerability in a widely used piece of hardware [S-043]. If the victim had known every device was vulnerable, they could have patched it.
*   **Dependencies & Regulations:** The proliferation of IoT devices in agriculture (sensors, drones) and food processing (smart meters, connected ovens) makes this a universal challenge [S-011, S-016]. The NIS2 directive requires entities to conduct risk assessments, which is impossible without a complete asset inventory.
*   **Plausible Consequences:** Without a full inventory, the borrower is flying blind. It cannot patch what it doesn't know exists, leaving a constantly growing attack surface exposed. This dramatically increases the probability of a successful breach.
*   **Non-Technical Follow-Up Questions:**
    1.  "Do you have a complete list of every single device that connects to your network, from executive laptops to factory floor sensors?"
    2.  "How do you account for new devices being added to the network by different departments without going through formal IT procurement?"
    3.  "How often do you perform a network scan to discover new or unauthorised devices?"
*   **Indicators for Specialist Review:** The borrower has never conducted a network-wide asset discovery scan or relies only on a static spreadsheet maintained by the IT department.

**PROV-004: Backup Strategy & Testing**

*   **Potential Borrower Weakness:** An adverse answer would indicate that the borrower's backups are either not performed correctly (e.g., not offline), not stored securely, or have never been tested for restoration.
*   **Related Threats/Incidents:** This is a key failure point in the face of ransomware. The choice to pay the ransom is often driven by the fear that backups are corrupt or will take too long to restore. The months-long disruption at Asahi Group Holdings suggests that their recovery process was exceptionally difficult [S-045].
*   **Dependencies & Regulations:** Backups are a key element of an effective incident response plan, which is a requirement under frameworks like NIST CSF and is implicitly required for resilience. The SEC rules require a disclosure of the incident's impact, which would include the ability to restore operations.
*   **Plausible Consequences:** Operationally, this leads to prolonged downtime, as the organisation is unable to recover its systems and data. Financially, this translates directly into lost revenue, increased costs for manual workarounds, and a higher likelihood of paying a ransom. It severely damages the borrower's operational resilience.

The Agriculture, Food and Tobacco (AFT) sector demonstrates a high inherent exposure to fraud, driven by a confluence of structural characteristics that create multiple avenues for criminal activity. The risk profile varies significantly across sub-sectors, from commodity trading desks to retail food distributors and agricultural production entities, yet the underlying structural drivers remain consistent throughout the farm-to-table value chain [S-063, S-067]. This analysis examines the sector's inherent fraud exposure through a typology screen, documented cases, quantitative evidence, structural drivers, and emerging threats, distinguishing between inherent structural vulnerability and observed preparedness.

A typology screen reveals that most common fraud types are highly material to the AFT sector, with many being either cyber-enabled or hybrid in nature. Payment diversion and invoice fraud are exceptionally prevalent due to the sector's high payment intensity; organisations frequently make payments to a vast and changing network of suppliers, including farmers, fertiliser companies, seed distributors, and transporters [S-063]. This environment facilitates "vendor email compromise" where attackers impersonate legitimate suppliers to divert payments to their own accounts [S-004]. Similarly, business email compromise (BEC) targeting finance departments to alter payment instructions is a significant threat, exacerbated by the urgency often associated with procurement in agriculture . Account takeover of ERP, banking, or supplier portals provides direct access to these high-value targets [S-004]. Insider fraud and asset misappropriation are also material, given the sector's reliance on cash, inventory, and high-value movable assets like livestock and harvested crops [S-070]. Financial statement fraud can be used to mask these activities or secure financing [S-071]. Corruption and bribery are heightened risks, particularly in commodity trading where large sums of money change hands and in public procurement for subsidies and grants [S-097, S-098]. Identity fraud, including synthetic identities, poses a threat to customer-facing operations, potentially leading to refund liabilities and conduct issues [S-082]. Finally, fraud against customers, such as food safety adulteration or economic substitution, is a root risk that occurs at multiple points in the supply chain and represents a significant liability [S-066, S-068].

## Q11 - Cyber / ICT Exposure

*Source: File B, Cyber/ICT Exposure Analysis. Conclusion: inherent cyber/ICT exposure is High (Provisional; confidence Medium); digitisation has outpaced security investment, IT/OT convergence and legacy devices widen the attack surface, and ransomware and nation-state targeting are rising.*

The Agriculture, Food and Tobacco (AFT) sector faces a rapidly escalating and materially elevated inherent cyber/ICT risk profile. This exposure stems from a fundamental mismatch: the sector's accelerated adoption of digital technologies, often termed "Agriculture 4.0," has outpaced corresponding investments in cybersecurity defences [S-044, S-013]. This has created a sprawling and largely insecure attack surface, characterised by a high dependency on legacy systems, the convergence of Information Technology (IT) and Operational Technology (OT) networks, and a growing reliance on a complex ecosystem of third-party vendors [S-044, S-087]. Consequently, the sector has become a prime target for a wide range of cyber adversaries, from financially motivated ransomware groups to nation-state actors with strategic interests in disrupting global food systems [S-004, S-013].

Digital dependency and technology intensity are now defining features of modern agribusiness. Precision farming relies on GPS-guided machinery, aerial imaging, and vast networks of IoT sensors to optimise yields [S-044]. Supply chain management involves sophisticated logistics platforms, and food processing plants depend on automated systems for quality control and packaging [S-063]. This digital transformation, while increasing efficiency, has made the sector highly sensitive to ICT disruptions. Any interruption to these systems can halt production, disrupt global supply chains, and cause immediate financial losses, making companies highly susceptible to ransom demands [S-087]. The IT/OT convergence is a key vulnerability. Historically separate IT networks (for business operations) and OT networks (for industrial control systems) are now increasingly interconnected, often through devices like Historians, which store process data and sit at the IT/OT boundary [S-075]. This convergence was identified as an initial access vector in 10% of OT incidents in 2024, providing attackers a pathway from the corporate network into key production environments [S-075]. Many of the connected devices, including building management systems, physical access controls, and industrial sensors, are "shadow OT" - installed by plant operators for convenience but never inventoried or secured by IT departments, creating blind spots in security posture [S-004].

The reliance on legacy systems and a heterogeneous technology landscape further compounds the risk. Many OT systems and IoT devices in the field were developed before modern cybersecurity threats emerged and are built on proprietary operating systems that receive infrequent or no security updates [S-076, S-087]. Their long device lifecycles mean they remain in service long after planned end-of-support dates, leaving them vulnerable to known exploits [S-076]. These devices are often resource-constrained, preventing the installation of traditional endpoint security agents, which forces organisations to rely on network-level visibility and segmentation - a method that is difficult to implement effectively across a diverse and decentralised environment [S-076]. Cloud reliance is growing, but migration is often gradual, leaving hybrid environments with complex configurations that are difficult to secure. Technology concentration is also a factor; a few key vendors, such as SAP, Oracle, and Microsoft, provide core ERP and processing platforms to many large agribusinesses, creating a focal point for attackers [S-072].

The cyber-enabled fraud attack surface is significant. Poor email authentication postures leave organisations vulnerable to BEC attacks, where executives are impersonated to authorise fraudulent wire transfers [S-004]. The public exposure of executive and finance staff data makes them prime targets for social engineering [S-082]. Susceptibility to voice or video impersonation is a growing concern, as AI-powered deepfake audio and video are being used to bypass security protocols [S-077]. Digital payment initiation and approval channels, often integrated into ERP systems, represent another key attack vector [S-004]. Vendor email compromise is a particularly effective technique, leveraging compromised supplier credentials to gain a foothold in a target organisation's network [S-004].

Cyber incident trends confirm the sector's growing vulnerability. Ransomware attacks targeting the AFT sector have surged dramatically. In the first half of 2026, incidents increased by 62% compared to the same period in 2025, with the Food and Ag-ISAC tracking 227 incidents through July 2026 [S-004]. In 2025, the sector accounted for 4.2% of the 6,377 ransomware incidents tracked by the ISAC, a significant figure for a sector that may be less digitally mature than others [S-077]. The Qilin ransomware group was the most active in 2026, launching 47 attacks, followed by The Gentlemen and Akira [S-004]. These groups are not indiscriminate; they specifically target the sector, exploiting vulnerabilities in widely used software platforms like Citrix NetScaler and Fortinet to gain initial access [S-077]. Nation-state actors are now the second-largest category of adversaries after ransomware groups [S-004]. China's cyber operators are engaged in sustained intelligence collection on U.S. agricultural biotechnology, seeds, and food production systems, likely for future conflict scenarios [S-004, S-013]. Russian state-sponsored groups have targeted essential infrastructure, including fertiliser logistics and grain cooperatives, while Iran has demonstrated a willingness to directly target food security by attacking programmable logic controllers (PLCs) in processing lines [S-004, S-013].

Sector cybersecurity maturity remains low relative to the scale of the threat. While large multinational agribusinesses may have invested in enterprise-grade security programmes, a systemic imbalance exists where smaller producers and cooperatives operate on thin margins and cannot afford equivalent protections [S-013]. This creates a weak link in the overall supply chain. There is no comprehensive federal strategy or modelling in the U.S. to determine the required investment to defend against nation-state threats, highlighting a major gap in national security planning [S-013]. Supervisory findings and independent research indicate recurring control weaknesses, including a lack of network segmentation, failure to patch known vulnerabilities in a timely manner, inadequate access controls for OT systems, and insufficient employee training on phishing and social engineering [S-084, S-087]. Preparedness for detection, containment, and recovery is often reactive rather than proactive. The formation of the Food and Ag-ISAC is a positive step toward improving collective defence and information sharing, but participation, especially among small and mid-size organisations, is a challenge [S-084].

Comparative analysis places the AFT sector in a high-risk category. The manufacturing vertical, which shares many technological characteristics with agriculture (e.g., OT systems, supply chains), ranked fifth among industry sectors with the riskiest connected devices on average, surpassed only by retail [S-075]. This indicates that the AFT sector's inherent technological exposure is comparable to other heavy industries undergoing digitalisation. Its risk profile is arguably higher than that of the pharmaceuticals sector, which operates in more controlled environments, but lower than pure-play tech or financial services, which are primary targets for all forms of cybercrime. The key differentiator for the AFT sector is the tangible, physical-world consequence of a cyberattack, such as compromised food safety, environmental contamination, or the complete halt of a production line, which elevates the severity of incidents beyond mere data loss or financial theft [S-084, S-013].

The inherent exposure of the Agriculture, Food and Tobacco (AFT) sector to operational disruptions is rated as **High**. This assessment is based on the sector's profound structural fragility, which arises from its very high sensitivity to interruptions across a globally interconnected, just-in-time supply chain; its growing concentration in key third-party providers for essential digital and logistical functions; and the physical vulnerability of its core assets. Unlike purely digital sectors, the AFT industry's resilience is challenged by a hybrid of physical and digital risks, where a cyberattack on an operational technology (OT) system can have immediate and severe consequences for production, safety, and distribution [S-087, S-013]. The sector's operating model, often characterised by decentralisation and thin profit margins, further constrains its ability to invest in robust business continuity and disaster recovery capabilities, amplifying the systemic impact of any major disruption [S-013].

Essential processes within the AFT sector are deeply embedded in a complex web of dependencies. The entire farm-to-table value chain - from upstream inputs like seeds and fertilisers to downstream retail and consumption - is a tightly coupled system [S-063]. Failure at any single point can trigger a cascade of disruptions throughout the network. Key triggers for such disruptions include supplier failures, transportation bottlenecks, adverse weather events, geopolitical conflicts, pandemics, and government actions [S-063, S-065]. For instance, a study identifying 43 distinct supply chain risks found that "Failure of the supplier to ensure a stable supply of raw materials" and "Infrastructure problems" were listed as key root risks affecting every stage of the supply chain [S-066]. The EU's contingency plans for ensuring food supply security explicitly recognise these vulnerabilities and the need for coordinated public-private approaches to manage crises [S-065]. The reliance on uninterrupted technology, outsourced operations (such as contract farming or third-party logistics), and essential infrastructure like energy grids makes the sector highly susceptible to both external shocks and internal failures [S-063].

Observed operational disruptions, many of which are cyber-enabled, vividly illustrate this inherent vulnerability. The ransomware attack on JBS, the world's largest meatpacker, in 2021 provides a stark example. The attack forced the temporary shutdown of 47 sites across Australia and nine in the USA, halting nearly one-fifth of U.S. beef production and threatening national meat supplies [S-088, S-013]. Similarly, a ransomware attack on Dole in 2023 led to a temporary shutdown of North American production facilities, disrupting grocery shipments and causing shortages of Dole products in stores [S-088]. Schreiber Foods faced a ransomware attack that shut down its milk processing plants and distribution centres over a weekend, forcing it to redirect milk transporters [S-088]. These incidents demonstrate how a single, targeted cyberattack can cripple production and distribution, leading to massive financial losses and supply chain chaos. The cost of such downtime is substantial; a survey by Claroty found that over one-third of respondents from the food and beverage sector estimated that an hour of operational downtime would cost them at least $1 million in revenue [S-087]. This economic reality explains why companies like JBS were willing to pay a multimillion-dollar ransom to restore operations quickly [S-088].

Third-party concentration presents another layer of systemic risk. While the AFT sector itself is highly fragmented, with thousands of small and medium-sized farms and processors, its essential digital and logistical functions are often outsourced to a handful of large providers. This mirrors the concentration risk that regulations like the EU's Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) were designed to mitigate in the financial sector [S-081, S-085]. Large agribusinesses and food manufacturers rely heavily on a few key vendors for core systems like Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP), supply chain management, and customer relationship management [S-004]. Cloud providers like Amazon Web Services, Google Cloud, and Microsoft Azure, designated as CTPPs (EU-designated ICT third-party providers under DORA; official term contains a restricted word) under DORA, are likely foundational to the IT infrastructure of many major players in the AFT sector [S-072]. Dependence on a single provider for these key functions creates a single point of failure. If such a provider suffers a major outage or a successful cyberattack, it could simultaneously impact a large portion of the sector, propagating the disruption far beyond the initial victim. The lack of readily available, drop-in alternatives for specialised services increases substitutability risk, making exit strategies from a failing provider very complex and time-consuming [S-073, S-087].

Evidence on business continuity and disaster recovery maturity is mixed. Large, well-capitalised corporations like JBS and Dole likely have sophisticated plans and testing regimes in place, as evidenced by their attempts to restore operations after an attack [S-088]. However, for the majority of smaller producers and processors, such capabilities are severely limited. Thin profit margins, which can be as low as 1.6% in food processing, constrain investment in resilience [S-087]. This creates a systemic weakness where the entire supply chain is only as strong as its least-resilient node. The formation of the Food and Ag-ISAC is a crucial initiative aimed at improving collective awareness and response capabilities, but it does not replace the need for individual organisational preparedness [S-084]. The propagation of a single incident across the sector is a significant concern. A cyberattack on a major logistics provider could disrupt the movement of goods for numerous food producers. A contamination event at a centralised ingredient supplier could affect thousands of finished products. The interdependencies are so deep that a localised problem can quickly escalate into a regional or even global crisis, impacting not only the AFT sector but also related sectors like retail, transportation, and healthcare [S-063, S-084].

Fraud through third parties is a prominent risk channel. Weak supplier onboarding and bank-detail change processes are common control failures that facilitate payment diversion and invoice fraud [S-004]. The use of intermediaries and subcontractor chains creates opacity, making it difficult to confirm legitimacy and detect fraudulent activities [S-067]. In a cyber-enabled scenario, a third-party vendor's network can serve as the initial access point for an attacker to infiltrate a target company's systems . The SolarWinds attack, while not AFT-specific, serves as a powerful illustration of how a supply chain compromise can have catastrophic, widespread consequences, impacting over 18,000 organisations globally [S-084]. The sector's reliance on a vast and often poorly vetted network of suppliers, agents, and contractors makes it particularly susceptible to this type of indirect attack.

## Q13 - Governance Structure & Operating Model Complexity

*Source: File B, Governance Structure and Operating Model Complexity Analysis. Conclusion: inherent exposure is Medium (Provisional; confidence Low); the driver is structural diversity (multinationals, cooperatives, family farms) and fragmented US oversight rather than pervasive misconduct.*

The inherent exposure of the Agriculture, Food and Tobacco (AFT) sector to governance and operating-model failures is assessed as **Medium**. This rating reflects the sector's considerable structural and operational complexity, which presents unique challenges for establishing and enforcing consistent risk management standards, rather than indicating a pervasive culture of misconduct or a high likelihood of governance failures. The sector is not monolithic; it comprises a diverse array of corporate structures - including large publicly traded multinationals, privately held family businesses, and cooperative models - that exhibit vastly different risk appetites, resource levels, and governance maturity [S-089, S-094]. This fragmentation, combined with a history of market power imbalances and a regulatory landscape that is often duplicated and agency-specific, creates a fractured defence against systemic risks [S-064, S-013]. While documented enforcement actions reveal specific instances of failure, the primary inherent risk lies in the difficulty of achieving uniform, resilient governance across such a heterogeneous industry.

The typical corporate structures within the AFT sector are a key driver of this complexity. At one end of the spectrum are large, vertically integrated corporations like Tyson Foods, which owns and controls every aspect of its production process, from feed suppliers to processing plants and distribution [S-089]. This model can create significant market power imbalances, giving dominant firms like the four largest US beef processors (which control 85% of the market) the ability to set unfavourable terms for smaller upstream producers [S-064, S-089]. At the other end are small-scale farms and processors, often organised as sole proprietorships or partnerships. A significant and unique segment of the sector, particularly in commodity production, consists of agricultural cooperatives [S-096]. Cooperatives are owned and controlled by their farmer-members, but this structure introduces unique governance challenges related to financial management, strategic decision-making, and accountability that differ from traditional investor-owned firms [S-094, S-095]. The existence of these disparate models makes it difficult to apply a single governance framework or expectation across the entire sector.

Outsourcing, intermediaries, and subcontracting are ubiquitous, further distributing operational responsibilities and complicating oversight. Large food processors often rely on third-party logistics providers, co-packers, and numerous local suppliers, creating a fragmented operational footprint [S-063]. This decentralisation, while efficient, can lead to inconsistent application of internal controls, risk management policies, and ethical standards across different parts of the organisation and its extended network [S-078]. Mergers and acquisitions add another layer of complexity, often resulting in legacy IT environments and fragmented technology ownership that are difficult to integrate securely and efficiently [S-080]. This complexity is not inherently a sign of weak governance, but it does increase the potential for control gaps and blind spots, particularly in areas like third-party risk management and data security.

While documented governance failures do occur, they are often specific to individual companies or practices rather than systemic to the entire sector. The US Department of Justice and FDA have brought numerous enforcement actions against AFT companies for significant lapses. For example, Family Dollar Stores LLC pleaded guilty to holding consumer products in insanitary conditions and agreed to a $41.675 million penalty related to a rodent-infested warehouse [S-091]. Covetrus pleaded guilty to criminal misbranding of veterinary drugs, and a New York veterinarian was sentenced for opioid theft, highlighting failures in product safety and handling [S-091]. The FDA's Center for Veterinary Medicine (CVM) issued multiple warning letters in spring 2026 for drug residue violations and unsanitary conditions, demonstrating persistent issues in a regulated segment of the sector [S-090]. These cases show that when failures occur, the consequences can be severe. However, they do not necessarily prove that the *inherent structural exposure* to governance failure is higher than in other heavily regulated sectors like pharmaceuticals or chemicals.

Supervisory findings and prosecutor actions do point to recurring issues related to accountability and internal controls. The repeated issuance of warning letters by the FDA for misbranding and Current Good Manufacturing Practice (CGMP) failures suggests a systemic difficulty in enforcing basic quality and safety controls across the industry [S-090, S-092]. The focus of enforcement on issues like undeclared allergens, unauthorised ingredients, and false health claims indicates that pressure to maximise profits can sometimes overshadow commitments to safety and transparency [S-092]. The concept of "tone from the top" is key here; a weak risk culture, where senior leadership prioritises growth over control effectiveness, can lead to long-standing compliance gaps that regulators eventually penalise [S-078]. The fact that some large companies face repeated warnings from auditors and regulators without decisive action is a classic signal of a weakening risk culture [S-078].

Aligning the AFT sector's governance with advanced external frameworks like Basel III or DORA is a forward-looking objective rather than a current state. Basel III's Principles for Sound Operational Risk Management (PSMOR) emphasise board-level ownership of risk appetite, robust control environments, and rigorous change management - all hallmarks of sound governance [S-080]. DORA similarly mandates strong governance, requiring competent authorities to oversee the risk management framework and hold senior management accountable [S-074, S-086]. While the AFT sector operates under a framework of food safety regulations like the FSMA, which emphasises a preventive control-centric approach, this framework is not yet fully harmonised with the principles of operational resilience and cyber resilience that underpin Basel and DORA [S-060, S-068]. The sector's approach to governance is therefore more focused on preventing specific types of harm (e.g., contamination) than on managing the broad spectrum of operational, financial, and cyber risks that threaten systemic stability.

Finally, the sector faces unique challenges in assigning responsibility. Shared technology platforms, essential suppliers, and complex subcontractor chains can obscure accountability for failures [S-073]. When a disruption occurs, it can be difficult to determine whether the fault lies with the producer, the supplier, the logistics provider, or the third-party software vendor. This ambiguity can delay response efforts and complicate liability assessments. The lack of a single, overarching regulator in the US - where responsibilities are spread across the USDA, FDA, EPA, and other agencies - exacerbates this issue, creating compliance burdens and potential gaps in oversight [S-013]. While complexity, size, or international reach alone are not proof of weak governance, the combination of structural diversity, regulatory fragmentation, and persistent enforcement issues in specific areas leads to a Medium inherent exposure rating for Governance Structure and Operating Model.

Comparing the Agriculture, Food and Tobacco (AFT) sector's inherent risk profile to structurally similar peer sectors is crucial for contextualising its exposures. The analysis focuses on four key dimensions - Fraud, Cyber/ICT, Operational Resilience/TPRM, and Governance - and benchmarks the AFT sector against peers such as **Manufacturing**, **Retail/Consumer Goods**, and **Pharmaceuticals**. This comparison highlights the unique vulnerabilities of the AFT sector, which stem from its hybrid nature as both a physical and a digital entity, its position at the base of the consumer supply chain, and its fragmented ownership structure.

In the **Fraud** dimension, the AFT sector's inherent exposure is assessed as **Higher** than that of Pharmaceuticals and broadly comparable to, or slightly higher than, Retail/Consumer Goods. The primary driver for this elevated risk is the sector's exceptional payment intensity and supplier density. With thousands of transactions occurring daily to a vast and often transient network of farmers, input suppliers, and transporters, the attack surface for payment diversion, invoice fraud, and supplier impersonation is immense [S-063, S-067]. This characteristic is less pronounced in the more vertically integrated and consolidated pharmaceutical industry. Compared to Retail, which also has high payment volumes, the AFT sector's reliance on a less standardised, more decentralised supplier base (especially in primary agriculture) may introduce greater opacity and control challenges. Furthermore, the AFT sector possesses a distinct and material exposure to public fund fraud (grants, subsidies), a risk that is less relevant to private-sector pharmaceutical and retail entities [S-068]. Peer data from investigating case studies shows that procurement fraud is a significant issue across multiple sectors, but the sheer scale of the AFT supply chain magnifies this risk [S-097].

For **Cyber/ICT**, the AFT sector's inherent exposure is rated as **Comparable to Manufacturing and Higher than Retail**. The two sectors are structurally similar in their use of OT systems, industrial control networks, and reliance on a complex supply chain of components and services [S-075, S-087]. Both face threats to their physical production processes from cyberattacks. The Forescout 2025 report ranked the manufacturing vertical fifth among industry sectors with the riskiest connected devices, behind only retail, underscoring the high baseline risk in this area [S-075]. The AFT sector's risk is arguably higher than that of Retail because its OT systems are often more physically dispersed and less centralised, making them harder to monitor and protect. Compared to Pharmaceuticals, which typically operates in more controlled, clean-room environments with less direct interaction with unsecured field devices, the AFT sector's risk is significantly higher. The key differentiator for the AFT sector is the direct physical-world consequence of a cyber incident. While a cyberattack on a retailer might result in lost sales or stolen customer data, an attack on an AFT facility can lead to spoiled inventory, compromised food safety, or environmental hazards, creating a more severe and tangible risk profile [S-084, S-013].

All three sectors rely on just-in-time models that are sensitive to disruption, but the AFT sector's assets are fundamentally different. Its assets - livestock, crops, soil - are geographically dispersed, exposed to uncontrollable external factors like weather and disease, and less easily recoverable from a catastrophic event [S-063, S-065]. A pandemic can wipe out a flock of livestock in a way it cannot instantly destroy a stockpile of retail goods. The sector's supply chain is also more linear and dependent on a few key nodes, such as major ports for grain exports or centralised processing hubs, creating chokepoints that are attractive targets [S-013]. While Retail and Logistics also face concentration risk with major carriers and e-commerce platforms, the AFT sector's resilience is structurally weaker due to the perishable and environmentally dependent nature of its core assets. The risk of a single point of failure is amplified by the convergence of IT and OT, where a cyberattack can have an immediate physical impact on production, a risk that is less direct in a purely commercial retail environment [S-087].

In the **Governance Structure and Operating Model** dimension, the AFT sector's inherent exposure is rated as **Higher than Manufacturing and Pharmaceuticals**. This is not due to a higher incidence of misconduct, but because of the sector's unique structural complexity. The blend of large, multinational corporations, small family farms, and legally distinct agricultural cooperatives creates a much more heterogeneous landscape than the relatively standardised corporate structures typical of Manufacturing and Pharmaceuticals [S-089, S-094]. This diversity makes it exceedingly difficult to implement and enforce uniform governance and risk management standards across the entire industry. The governance challenges of a cooperative in Iowa are fundamentally different from those of a multinational meat processor in Brazil, yet both fall under the same broad sector classification. This contrasts with sectors like Banking or Insurance, which have more homogenous legal and operational structures. Furthermore, the AFT sector suffers from a fragmented regulatory landscape in key jurisdictions like the US, where multiple agencies with overlapping mandates create compliance burdens and potential gaps in oversight, a problem less prevalent in more singularly-regulated industries [S-013]. This structural heterogeneity and regulatory fragmentation create a higher inherent exposure to governance-related risks, such as inconsistent implementation of controls and difficulty in assigning accountability for systemic failures.

### Main Gaps and Limitations

This assessment is subject to several significant gaps and limitations:

1.  **Lack of Comprehensive Quantitative Data:** The most important gap is the absence of reliable, sector-specific data on the frequency, typology, and financial impact of fraud and cyber incidents. The analysis relies heavily on proxies from regulatory alerts (FDA, EU ACN) and anecdotal evidence from high-profile cases. This limits the precision of the risk assessment and caps the confidence in the ratings.
2.  **Subsector Variation:** The analysis treats the "Agriculture, Food and Tobacco" sector as a broad category. A more granular analysis would reveal vastly different risk profiles - for example, a global commodity trader versus a local organic dairy farmer. The user's directive to analyse the sector as a whole necessitates this broad-brush approach, which inherently masks these key differences.
3.  **Reporting Bias:** There is a clear under-reporting bias for cyber incidents, as acknowledged by industry experts who state that most ransomware attacks are not reported [S-005]. This means the true extent of cyber risk is likely substantially higher than what is captured in public reports from ISACs or other sources.
4.  **Inherent vs. Observed Risk:** This report is an assessment of *inherent* exposure - the risk before considering any mitigating controls. It does not evaluate the effectiveness of a specific borrower's controls, which would require a separate audit or engagement. A borrower with strong controls may warrant a lower risk rating than this inherent assessment suggests.
5.  **Jurisdictional Variability:** While an attempt has been made to cover the EU, UK, and US, the regulatory landscape is complex and constantly evolving. Specific rules and enforcement priorities can vary significantly between jurisdictions, and this report captures a snapshot in time.

**Fraud / Cyber split (merge note; ratings unchanged):** File B does not state how cyber-enabled fraud is split between dimensions. For use of these ratings: the access mechanism (ransomware, intrusion, account compromise) is recorded under Cyber/ICT; the deception and resulting loss (BEC, invoice and payment diversion, supplier impersonation, subsidy fraud) under Fraud. JBS and Dole are cited as evidence in both RAT-2 and RAT-3 and appear as FRD-C1 and FRD-C2; their losses are counted once.

**Merge notes:** (a) The fraud confidence cap cites an EBA operational risk page [S-083] (C-24). (b) RAT-3 cites "JBS and Sysco incidents" as cascading supply-chain effects; Sysco is described as a data breach (C-20). (c) "Downtime costing millions per hour" in RAT-3 overstates the survey finding in Q12 (C-15). (d) The ratings rest on drivers present in the merged registers (Section 4.13). (e) File B's gaps 1 to 5 are carried into Section 8 (G-05 to G-09).
