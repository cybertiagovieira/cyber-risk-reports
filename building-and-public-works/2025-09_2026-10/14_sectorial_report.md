# Building & Public Works - Sectorial Cyber Risk Intelligence

**Report date:** 14 October 2026. **Reporting period:** 1 September 2025 to 14 October 2026. **Evidence cut-off:** 14 October 2026. **Rolling evidence horizon:** 18 months for patterns and public loss cases.

**Geography:** Global, with primary focus on the European Union (EU), European Economic Area (EEA) and United Kingdom (UK); North American and Asian evidence used for comparison. **Audience:** First Line of Defence (1LoD) operational management and Risk Committee. **Boundary:** public external data only; no internal, client or bank-specific information is used or implied. **Edition:** baseline edition; no prior publication exists, so changes are described against the period rather than against an earlier report.

**Evidence labels used throughout:** Observed (external fact from a named source), Benchmark (sector statistic with stated population), Proxy (indirect or derived measure), Judgement (analytic judgement by this report), Assumption (scenario assumption), Action (management recommendation). Each statement is preceded by its source reference in the form [Source/Title][n]; full details are in the References section.

## Sector Exposure & Executive Risk Outlook

### Bottom line

[RBKC - Incident summary and recovery plans][5] [IMY - Sanction against Miljödata][10] [Procore - FY2025 Form 10-K][27] [Dragos - Industrial ransomware Q2 2026][20] The best-documented Building & Public Works (B&PW) cyber propagation pathway in the period runs through the shared digital layers the sector depends on: public-owner shared services, sector software platforms, cloud hosting, materials suppliers and payment chains; leak-site data nevertheless show many more construction ransomware claims than public disclosures, so direct attacks on firms remain numerous. [RBKC - Cyber attack recovery update March 2026][6] [RBKC - Cyber security attack FAQs][8] The best-documented disruption hit a public-sector project owner: planning was unavailable for about 15 weeks, revenues and benefits for about six months, and new invoice processing was constrained (existing invoices were paid through a controlled run; the length of the payment constraint was not disclosed). [Femern A/S - FLC cyber incident notice][1] [Copenhagen Post - Fehmarn Belt contractor incident][2] In October 2026 a cyber incident at the main contractor of the Fehmarnbelt tunnel indicated, on media-reported and unconfirmed effects, that the same pathway can reach marine and site operations. [SANKO TECHNO - TSE ransomware disclosure][15] Public financial disclosure remains thin: no contractor or supplier in the period has published a quantified cyber cost, delay or contractual effect. For a bank, the relevance is conditional and indirect - through contractor working capital, owner payment delays, supplier insolvency, guarantees and emergency lending - not through any asserted exposure of a specific institution.

### Principal judgements

**1. Shared digital layers are the best-documented propagation pathway (Judgement; confidence medium).** [RBKC - Incident summary and recovery plans][5] [RBKC - Audit and Transparency Committee pack September 2026][7] [IMY - Sanction against Miljödata][10] [BleepingComputer - Miljödata fine][11] [Procore - FY2025 Form 10-K][27] The two most fully documented in-period disruptions propagated across many dependent organisations: a shared IT estate serving three London boroughs, and a software supplier used by a majority of Swedish municipalities (regulator) and, per secondary reporting, also regions, state agencies and private companies (Tier 3); at the same time one construction-management platform reports 17,850 customers in about 160 countries hosted on third-party cloud. **Competing explanation:** public bodies must publish governance records while private contractors rarely do, so owner-side cases may be over-represented; leak-site data show many more construction ransomware claims than public disclosures. **What would change this judgement:** disclosure of large contractor-level disruptions with quantified programme impact exceeding owner-side effects.

**2. Time-sensitive site operations turn IT outages into schedule loss (Judgement; confidence medium).** [QBE and Control Risks - From blueprints to breaches][18] [QBE - UK construction project delivery release][19] Insurer research reports that ransomware incidents in construction averaged 24 days of downtime in 2025 and that delays drive cost escalation, contractual disputes and subcontractor effects. [Femern A/S - FLC cyber incident notice][1] [Copenhagen Post - Fehmarn Belt contractor incident][2] The Fehmarnbelt owner confirmed the contractor incident, while media reported, without owner confirmation, that vessels stopped and digital access control failed. **What would change this judgement:** evidence that contract float and manual workarounds routinely absorb outages without programme loss.

**3. Exposed public-works operational technology is an active pressure point (Judgement; confidence medium-low).** [Canadian Cyber Centre - Iranian threat bulletin][25] Following the US/Israel strikes of February 2026, a national authority judged Iranian cyber retaliation very likely and warned of opportunistic targeting of poorly secured infrastructure networks and internet-connected devices. [MNIT - Statewide response, 28 July 2026][12] [MNIT - Continued response, 30 July 2026][13] In July 2026 more than 30 Minnesota community water systems experienced malicious activity on programmable logic controllers (PLCs) and human-machine interfaces (HMIs); the state made no attribution. No baseline exists to show a rise, and the activity may reflect opportunistic or criminal scanning rather than state direction (competing explanation). Construction firms are more likely to be collateral or access-path victims than primary targets. **What would change this judgement:** official attribution of the activity, or evidence that it is untargeted scanning only.

**4. Financial-system transmission is conditional and runs through liquidity (Judgement; confidence medium).** [Insolvency Service via The Gazette - July 2026 statistics][31] Construction recorded the highest number of company insolvencies in England and Wales in the 12 months to July 2026 (3,841 cases, 17% of cases with industry recorded); this is a count, not a rate, and is context for counterparty fragility rather than evidence of thin buffers. [UK Government - JLR loan guarantee][29] The UK Government's September 2025 guarantee of a commercial bank loan of up to GBP 1.5 billion to support a manufacturer's supply chain after a cyber stoppage shows how such shocks can reach bank lending. **What would change this judgement:** evidence that large contractors' liquidity buffers routinely absorb cyber-driven delays.

**5. The regulatory perimeter for construction is indirect but tightening (Judgement; confidence high).** [EUR-Lex - NIS2 Directive][32] [EUR-Lex - CER Directive][34] [legislation.gov.uk - NIS Regulations 2018][41] Construction is not a listed sector under the EU Network and Information Security Directive 2 (NIS2), the EU CER Directive (Directive (EU) 2022/2557 on entity resilience) or the UK NIS Regulations 2018; obligations reach contractors through owners' supply-chain duties, procurement terms, product rules and data-protection law. [European Commission - CRA reporting][36] [Council of the EU - Public Procurement Act proposal][38] Two in-period changes add pressure: Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) manufacturer reporting from 11 September 2026, and the 9 September 2026 proposal for an EU Public Procurement Act with cybersecurity and resilience provisions. **What would change this judgement:** national NIS2 measures that designate construction entities directly.

### Financial-system relevance

The sector matters to a bank through generic, evidence-supported channels: (a) contractor and subcontractor working capital, which depends on owner certification and payment processes that the RBKC case shows can be constrained by an owner outage (duration of the payment constraint not disclosed); (b) supplier fragility, for which construction's leading insolvency count (a count, not a rate) is context; (c) payment-chain fraud, which law enforcement links specifically to construction invoicing; and (d) emergency liquidity support for supply chains, as in the JLR guarantee precedent. These are conditional transmission channels and not statements about any institution's exposure, losses, capital or Risk Appetite Framework (RAF) position.

## Key Threats & Top 5 Cyber Attacks

### Selection criteria

Named incidents qualify only if (1) the event or its regulatory outcome falls inside 1 September 2025 to 14 October 2026; (2) existence is supported by a Tier 1 or Tier 2 source; (3) the affected organisation belongs to a B&PW subsector in scope; and (4) the case adds a distinct operational lesson. Leak-site victim claims without primary corroboration are excluded. Five incidents meet the standard. Threat pathways are shown separately and are patterns, not attacks.

### Threat pathways (patterns)

| Pathway (pattern) | Operational vulnerability | Systemic or concentration translation | Evidence base |
|---|---|---|---|
| P1 Ransomware and data extortion | Encryption and exfiltration halt ERP, scheduling, site access and document control | Many contractors and materials suppliers share a small number of criminal groups and access methods | Dragos Q2 2026; Black Kite 2026; QBE 2026 |
| P2 Identity and edge-access compromise | Phishing, VPNs without multi-factor authentication (MFA), unpatched edge and backup servers | One compromised identity provider or remote-access path opens many projects and joint ventures | ENISA 2025 (phishing about 60%, exploitation 21.3%); Akira advisory 2025 |
| P3 Shared IT and software-as-a-service (SaaS) dependency | Owner shared services and sector platforms are single points of failure | One supplier incident propagates to many owners, contractors and data subjects | RBKC 2025-26; Miljödata 2025-26; Procore 10-K |
| P4 Operational technology (OT), connected field and building systems | Internet-exposed PLCs and HMIs, weak passwords, poor IT/OT segregation | State-aligned and hacktivist actors can reach public-works assets across many small operators | Minnesota 2026; Bremanger 2025 (contextual); QBE 81% segmentation finding |
| P5 Payment-chain fraud | Supplier email compromise and bank-detail replacement on invoices | High-value, frequent stage payments create repeatable fraud opportunity across supply chains | AFP 2025 alert |

[ENISA - Threat Landscape 2025][23] Across 4,875 EU incidents (July 2024 to June 2025), phishing accounted for about 60% of observed initial access and vulnerability exploitation for 21.3%; OT threats were 18.2% and supply-chain threats 10.6% of threat categories (Observed). [CISA and partners - Akira advisory update][22] The November 2025 Akira advisory update reports about USD 244.17 million in proceeds, entry through VPNs without MFA, SonicWall and Cisco vulnerabilities and unpatched Veeam backup servers, and exfiltration in just over two hours in some cases; construction is not among the sectors it names (Observed). [Dragos - Industrial ransomware Q2 2026][20] Dragos reports that suppliers of building materials, construction services and equipment were repeatedly targeted because of ERP reliance, distributed locations and tight project timelines (Observed). [QBE and Control Risks - From blueprints to breaches][18] QBE and Control Risks report that inadequate IT/OT segmentation contributed to 81% of OT incident cases in 2025 (Dragos data) and that ransomware groups affected 526 construction organisations in 2025 (Benchmark). [AFP - Construction BEC alert][26] The Australian Federal Police (AFP) warned that criminals target construction firms for business email compromise (BEC) because of high-value, frequent transactions, replacing bank details on supplier invoices (Observed).

## Financial Impact: Building & Public Works

### Bottom line

Public financial evidence for B&PW cyber incidents is sparse and heterogeneous. The only in-period quantified costs come from one public owner and one regulator; no contractor or supplier has disclosed a cyber-driven cost, delay or contractual outcome. The dominant economic mechanism suggested by the evidence is time: weeks of downtime in delivery operations and months of disruption in owner approval processes, with constrained (duration undisclosed) payment processing.

### Financial-system transmission (conditional channels)

- **Owner payment and certification delay.** [RBKC - Incident summary and recovery plans][5] At RBKC, new invoices and routine payments were constrained and a controlled payment run processed existing invoices (Observed). Judgement: when owners cannot certify or pay, contractors and subcontractors draw on working-capital facilities; the bank-relevant signal is unusual drawdown or overdue receivables from public counterparties.
- **Supplier fragility and default.** [Insolvency Service via The Gazette - July 2026 statistics][31] Construction leads insolvency counts in England and Wales (Observed). [SC Media - Small construction firm collapse][45] Secondary reporting describes an unnamed small construction firm that closed months after ransomware destroyed its data and only backup (Tier 3, illustrative only). Judgement: cyber shocks can accelerate default among thinly capitalised subcontractors.
- **Emergency liquidity for supply chains.** [UK Government - JLR loan guarantee][29] The JLR guarantee was structured as a commercial bank loan backed by UK Export Finance covering typically up to 80% of lender risk, explicitly to support suppliers (Observed). Judgement: a comparable B&PW event affecting a tier-1 contractor would plausibly transmit through supplier payment terms and bank facilities rather than through direct losses.
- **Payment-chain fraud.** [AFP - Construction BEC alert][26] AFP reported AUD 152.6 million stolen through BEC in 2024 across Australian businesses, with construction specifically targeted (Observed; economy-wide figure). Judgement: banks carry operational and customer-outcome exposure through payment-redirection fraud in contractor payment flows.
- **Guarantees, bonds and insurance (analytic).** Judgement, without direct public case evidence: delay-driven claims can interact with performance bonds, advance-payment guarantees and liquidated damages; no in-period public case quantifies this channel.

## Financial Impact: Worst-Case Scenarios

The three scenarios below are structured stress narratives, not forecasts or loss estimates for any institution. No probabilities are assigned because no defensible external basis exists.

### Scenario 1 - Common digital-dependency failure

- **Trigger (Assumption).** [Procore - FY2025 Form 10-K][27] [QBE and Control Risks - From blueprints to breaches][18] A multi-day availability failure of a common service - a widely used construction-management or common-data-environment platform, its identity provider or its hosting cloud region - without data compromise.
- **Affected service.** Drawings, requests for information (RFIs), permits-to-work, site diaries, valuations and payment applications.
- **Propagation pathway.** [Procore - FY2025 Form 10-K][27] One platform reports 17,850 customers in about 160 countries and reliance on third-party data centres such as AWS, with customers having low tolerance for interruptions (Observed); a shared outage hits many contractors, owners and consultants at once.
- **Impact drivers.** [QBE and Control Risks - From blueprints to breaches][18] In a 2023 survey cited by QBE, 77% of respondents said they could tolerate no more than five days without project documentation (Benchmark, low confidence).
- **Financial transmission.** Delayed valuations and certifications, slower cash conversion for contractors, delay-entitlement disputes, and concurrent drawdown on bank facilities across many borrowers in the same sector.
- **Early signposts.** Provider status incidents; regional cloud impairments; credential-phishing waves against platform users. [The Register - AWS UAE drone strikes][28] Secondary reporting of AWS notices in March 2026 described drone strikes that impaired two of three UAE availability zones, with customers advised to move workloads (Tier 3; illustrative of physical regional loss, not used as a finding).
- **Limiting conditions.** Offline copies, alternative channels, multi-region hosting and contract float.
- **Management questions.** Which shared platforms and identity providers do the sector's largest counterparties depend on, and is there evidence of tested offline continuity for valuation and payment processes?

### Scenario 2 - Targeted operational disruption

- **Trigger (Assumption).** [Femern A/S - FLC cyber incident notice][1] [MNIT - Continued response, 30 July 2026][13] Ransomware or OT intrusion at a tier-1 contractor joint venture or a public-works operator during a time-sensitive programme phase, lasting one to four weeks (duration band informed by the 24-day QBE average; illustrative).
- **Affected service.** Site access control, scheduling, marine positioning and tunnelling operations, water and public-works plant, and building-management environments.
- **Propagation pathway.** [Copenhagen Post - Fehmarn Belt contractor incident][2] [Femern A/S - FLC cyber incident notice][1] Precautionary isolation of IT stops access control and coordination; in the Fehmarnbelt case media reported idle vessels (Observed, unconfirmed). [Havtil - PST dam intrusion notice][17] [QBE and Control Risks - From blueprints to breaches][18] For OT, weak passwords and poor IT/OT separation allowed direct manipulation of a dam's outflow in 2025 (contextual).
- **Impact drivers.** Missed weather or marine windows, standby costs, safety stand-downs, liquidated-damages exposure and regulatory notifications.
- **Financial transmission.** Contractor margin erosion and claims, bond and guarantee calls in disputes, insurer involvement, and pressure on subcontractors' liquidity.
- **Early signposts.** Contractor incident notices; idle-site or vessel reports; state or national cyber-agency alerts on internet-exposed OT; hacktivist claims.
- **Limiting conditions.** [AP via KFYR - Minnesota attribution report][14] Manual operation and segregated backups can restore service quickly; one Minnesota town restarted its plant within about 90 minutes (Observed).
- **Management questions.** For major infrastructure counterparties, is there public evidence of OT segregation, tested manual fallback for programme-time-sensitive operations and incident-notification terms with owners?

### Scenario 3 - Third- or fourth-party supply-chain concentration

- **Trigger (Assumption).** [IMY - Sanction against Miljödata][10] [Dragos - Industrial ransomware Q2 2026][20] A compromise that spreads through a shared supplier's access, data or payment relationships - a public-sector software supplier, a managed service provider, a building-materials manufacturer or a payment counterparty.
- **Affected service.** Owner HR, health-and-safety and planning systems; materials supply; invoicing and payment processes.
- **Propagation pathway.** [IMY - Sanction against Miljödata][10] [BleepingComputer - Miljödata fine][11] The Miljödata compromise reached a majority of Swedish municipalities (regulator) and, per secondary reporting, regions, state agencies and companies, and led the regulator to review customers as well as the supplier (Observed).
- **Impact drivers.** Simultaneous data-breach notifications, supervisory reviews of customers, materials delivery interruption and payment-diversion fraud.
- **Financial transmission.** Correlated delay across programmes that share a supplier; fraud losses in payment flows; supplier insolvency where recovery is prolonged.
- **Early signposts.** Supplier breach notices; regulator investigations of customers; leak-site claims against materials suppliers; BEC alerts.
- **Limiting conditions.** [SANKO TECHNO - TSE ransomware disclosure][15] Network segregation can contain a supplier incident to one subsidiary, as SANKO TECHNO reported (Observed).
- **Management questions.** Which suppliers serve a large share of public owners or contractors in a market, and do counterparties hold exit, fallback and data-segregation arrangements?

### Scenario distinctness

| Dimension | Scenario 1 | Scenario 2 | Scenario 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Point of failure | Availability of a common platform or cloud | One major contractor or operator | Compromise spreading through a supplier's access, data or payments |
| Breadth | Many firms at once | One programme or asset, deep effect | Many customers of one supplier |
| Main cost driver | Information and payment delay | Site and programme delay | Correlated delay, notifications, fraud |
| Key limiting condition | Offline continuity | Manual fallback and OT segregation | Supplier diversity and segregation |

### Sector lesson and read-across

- For public owners: shared-service models concentrate risk across authorities; recovery planning should prioritise planning consent, repairs ordering and supplier payment because these drive contractor activity.
- For contractors: owner-side outages can delay consents and instructions for months and constrain payment processing; contracts and cash-flow planning should treat owner digital disruption as a foreseeable event.
- For banks (generic): public-counterparty payment delays can surface as working-capital stress in contractors that are otherwise performing.

### What the case does not establish

The case does not show any quantified effect on contractors, any project delay attributable to the attack, the initial access vector, the insurance recovery, or that the same pattern applies to private contractors. It concerns one UK local-authority shared service.

## Methodology and Evidence Limitations

**Method.** Wide discovery across construction, civil engineering, public works, project owners, materials, construction technology and building systems, using English, German, French, Danish and Japanese queries, followed by primary-source research. Sources were classified as Tier 1 (official, legal, regulatory, affected organisation, government), Tier 2 (independent research with disclosed method) or Tier 3 (media and discovery). Tier 3 sources are never the sole support for a material finding; where Tier 3 detail is shown it is labelled. Publication date and observation period are recorded separately; repeated reporting of one incident is counted once. All sources were accessed on 14 October 2026.

**Evidence limitations.**

- Disclosure asymmetry: public bodies publish governance records while private contractors rarely disclose incidents, so public-owner cases are over-represented.
- No in-period contractor or supplier disclosed a quantified cost, delay or contractual effect; absence of disclosure is not evidence of no loss.
- The Fehmarnbelt evidence is one to two days old; operational effects are reported by media and unconfirmed.
- Leak-site benchmarks (Dragos, Black Kite) measure claims made by criminal groups, not sector attack rates, and use different windows and taxonomies; they are not combined.
- QBE downtime and survey figures have partially undisclosed methods.
- A Tier 1 measure of the sector's share of EU output and employment, and EU bank exposure to construction by sector code, could not be retrieved in this run.
- No in-period Tier 1 or Tier 2 incident involving building-management or building-automation systems was identified; the subsector is covered through product regulation and OT control evidence only.
- Legal article content for NIS2, CER, CRA and GDPR was recorded from the official texts; session access confirmed the official metadata pages.
- Minnesota attribution to Iran is preliminary and anonymous; the state has made no attribution.
- No numerical probabilities are assigned; scenarios are stress narratives.

**Confidence.** Confidence is high for regulatory status and for the RBKC and Miljödata facts, medium for the principal judgements, and low for scenario durations and the Fehmarnbelt operational effects.
